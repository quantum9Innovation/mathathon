8. WHY THESE PARTICULAR MOMENTS ARE DETERMINATE

Set Z_p=product_(j>=1)(1+p^(-j)), C_p=2Z_p. Then
F_r<=C_p p^(r(r-1)/2). For ANY limiting law with these moments, Markov
at r=k yields P(K>=k)<=C_p p^(-k(k+1)/2). If two laws have the same
moments, their coefficient differences a_k obey twice this bound, so
f(z)=sum a_k z^k is entire and vanishes at 1,p,p^2,... .

At R_n=p^(n+1/2), completion of the square gives

 max_|z|=R_n |f(z)|<=C'_p p^(n^2/2),

since the remaining discrete Gaussian sum has a uniform finite bound.
If nonzero f has order m at the origin, apply Jensen to f/z^m. Its
known n+1 nonzero zeros contribute exactly

 sum_(r=0)^n log_p(R_n/p^r)=(n+1)^2/2.

Thus max|f|>=|a_m|p^(m(n+1/2)+(n+1)^2/2). Comparing with the upper
bound leaves (m+1)n+(m+1)/2 in the exponent, a contradiction as n grows.
Other interior zeros only improve this bound. Zeros on the circle are
handled by the limiting version of Jensen, since the specified zeros
are strictly inside. This is a genuine determinacy argument at the
needed quadratic tail, not a general assertion that p-power moments
always determine a law.
