                                        Separator theorems are cornerstones of modern algorithmic graph theory. They facilitate divide-and-conquer
                                        algorithms on graphs, a basic technique in algorithm design. Furthermore, research in separator theorems often
                                        reveals deep and unexpected connections between different areas of math and computer science, some of which
                                        we will mention here.

and bounds: either the running time is too big (even for a fixed h) or the separator size is too big. Achieving the
best of both is left as an open problem by Bonnet et al. [BKL+ 26]:

Question 1. Can we construct a balanced separator of (nearly) optimal size in time O(poly(h)n) for Kh -minor-free
graphs?

    At the conceptual level, Question 1 asks for a unification of the two long lines of work on separators for
Kh -minor-free graphs over the last few decades.
    Beyond minor-free graphs, Plotkin, Rao, and Smith [PRS94] introduced the notion of shallow minor-free
graphs. Roughly speaking, Kh is an ℓ-shallow minor of G if the minor model of Kh in G can be formed from
(disjoint) subgraphs of radius at most ℓ. Plotkin, Rao, and Smith [PRS94] adapted the separator theorem of
Alon, Seymour, Thomas [AST90] to show that ℓ-shallow Kh -minor-free graphs have balanced separators of size
O(n/ℓ + ℓh2 log n). The concept of shallow minors and the Plotkin-Rao-Smith separator theorem are the basis
of the (award-winning) theory of graph sparsity by Nešetřil and Ossona de Mendez [NOdM12]. One key result,
among others, in this theory is a precise characterization of the classes of graphs that have strongly sublinear
separators of the form O(n1−α ) for a constant α ∈ (0, 1) [NOdM12, DN16, ER18]: these are exactly graphs with
polynomial expansion1 .

    We discuss the online learning algorithm first. To design our algorithms, we introduce a new approach based
on the follow the regularized leader (FTRL) framework, a well-studied technique in online learning and convex
optimization. For readers who are not familiar with online learning and FTRL, we give a very short primer in
Section 3.1 by outlining basic ideas in the FTRL framework. A very short summary is: learning happens in T
rounds, where in each round t, a learner has to output a vector x t ∈ X ⊆ Rd , and suffers a loss ℓ t (x t ) for a loss
function ℓ t : X → (−∞, +∞] that the learner does not know. (X is the domain.) In future rounds, the learner
gets access not just to the previous losses, but also their gradients. The learner’s goal is to minimize regret, defined
as the difference between the learner’s cumulative loss and the cumulative loss of the best fixed vector in hindsight.
The FTRL framework would tell the learner how to choose x t based on the previous choices x 1 , . . . , x t−1 and the
loss functions at previous steps ℓ1 (x), . . . , ℓ t−1 (x). In online learning, the loss functions are chosen by an online
adversary. FTRL itself is a generalization of many popular online learning algorithms, including multiplicative
weight update and matrix multiplicative weight update. However, the specific instantiation of FTRL that we will
employ is not captured by these special cases.
    Essentially any constrained convex optimization problem can be set up as a two-player min-max game via
Lagrangian duality, so online learning is also frequently employed as a method for solving convex optimization
problems. Since solutions to convex relaxations of combinatorial problems can often be rounded to approximate
solutions of the original combinatorial problems, one may expect that this is the methodology with which online
learning is employed for approximation algorithms. However, doing things in this manner does not make use
of the special characteristics of online learning. Instead, many fast approximation algorithms use the idea of
early termination: Perform a learning game over the course of (at most) T rounds between a learner and an
adversary, where the adversary has the option of terminating the entire game prematurely and outputting a
desired combinatorial solution (see e.g. [AK16, LTW24, KS26b]). In such games (including all the examples),
the adversary will also often use loss functions with some helpful properties like sparse gradients, in order to
reduce the computational overhead of the learner. This is often done in order to reduce the time complexity of the
learner from a large polynomial to near-linear (per-round). If the learning game completes all T rounds, such
approaches then certify a lower bound on the value of the problem. These lower bounds are guaranteed via duality


                                                                      4
and standard regret guarantees. Our algorithm shares a number of similar ideas with such works. However, unlike
the cited works, our objective will not simply follow as a natural convex relaxation of a combinatorial problem. In
fact, as previously mentioned, it will not be convex at all, which means duality and standard regret guarantees
will not help us in proving our results.
     At a high level, our goal, which is also our major conceptual contribution, is to set up an online learning game
with early termination such that, for a suitable choice of T and the loss functions, learning over T rounds yields an
early termination with a balanced separator in one of the rounds. We break this goal into three concrete steps.
(Step 1) Formulating a (new) continuous optimization problem Fµ,ι (λ) that captures the balanced separators. (For
now, the parameters µ and ι are not important.) Our formulation also relies on flow-cut duality. Unlike typical
optimization problems solved via FTRL, the variable λ in our problem has an exponentially large dimension, and
Fµ,ι (λ) is non-convex. (Step 2) Modeling the continuous optimization problem as an online learning problem.
(Step 3) Implementing each round of the online learning using the FTRL framework. As we mentioned in Step
1, our optimization has an exponential number of dimensions, so we cannot simply follow the standard FTRL
template, specifically the step of designing the loss function ℓ t . Here, we simplify the problem greatly to derive a
proxy (and simpler) optimization problem Gµ,ι (λ). A key technical innovation that we want to highlight here is
that we can relate ∇Gµ,ι (λ), the gradient of Gµ,ι (λ), to the (vertex-weighted) graph radius, from which we can
implement each FTRL round by doing a simple low-diameter decomposition on the input graphs. We will give a
more detailed exposition of these steps in Section 1.2 below.
     We mentioned above that the linear-time algorithm for minor-free graphs by Bonnet et al. [BKL+ 26] is obtained
through a novel technique that assigns weights to the graph’s vertices. As the algorithm proceeds through Θ(h2 )
iterations, it adjusts the vertices’ weights and outputs the separator in one of the iterations. Their vertex-weighting
scheme is perhaps the most innovative part of their paper, yet also the most mysterious. Our paper is inspired by
this weighting scheme, but we go one step further: by making a connection to the FTRL framework, the weighting
scheme arises naturally from Step 3, where we design the loss function ℓ t . Our resulting weighting scheme is
indeed simpler and more efficient: we can improve the running time from O(h13 n) [BKL+ 26] to O(h7 (m + n))
                                                                                p
without any additional effort, while improving the separator size from O(h13 n) [BKL+ 26] to nearly optimal as in
Theorem 2.


1.2    Technical overview
We now describe the ideas leading to our algorithm, which uses a learning game with early termination.

Step 1: Designing an objective. Our objective will be inspired by three related concepts: An existing probabilistic
path bound, subset flow problems, and the stochastic connectors of Bonnet et al. [BKL+ 26].
    Our starting point is a key component of Korhonen and Lokshtanov’s proof of Theorem 1. They gave a bound
on a vertex-variant of the L∞ -concurrent flow problem studied by Leighton and Rao [LR99], which routes one unit

almost all these prior bounds were based on various combinatorial methods and were inherently suboptimal as
a result. Korhonen and Lokshtanov instead used a probabilistic construction, essentially proving the following
intermediate result.

Step 2: Formulating an online learning problem. We formulate the two players of the learning game as follows.
In each round t, the learner plays a vector λ t of weights over all wide connectors, and the adversary responds with
a loss function. We constrain the game so that, at each round t, λ t has weight updates induced by at most one
new wide connector (in fact, up to normalization only one entry changes), or the game is terminated early with a
small balanced separator. If too many rounds complete (O(h2 ) for Kh -minor-free graphs, O(|V (H)| + |E(H)|) for
H-induced-minor-free graphs), an application of either Proposition 2 or Proposition 1 will be used to prove that
the graph contains a minor or induced-minor, respectively.

Step 3: Implementing each round of the online learning problem using the FTRL framework. It remains
to discuss several implementation details. First, during the course of the algorithm, we maintain the list of
wide connectors C t played so far, as well as a load vector p t for each one, containing the probability of being
an element of a sampled path (according to a certain distribution). We also maintain a prefix sum vector q t of
load vectors. At each iteration, the adversary uses these values to define a weight vector w t over the vertices,
inducing a vertex-weighted shortest-path metric (equivalently, an edge-weighted shortest-path metric where each
edge uv is given weight 12 (w t [u] + w t [v])). Using this metric, we will leverage a form of the flow-cut gap for
induced-minor-free graphs. Davies, Rz ˛   ażewski, Spalding-Jamieson, and Walczak recently proved the following
result.

    1. a vertex x ∈ V with |B(x, 11δ)| > η|V |;
    2. vertex sets A, B ⊆ V with |A| > ε|V |, |B| > ε|V |, and d(A, B) > δ;
    3. an induced minor model of the 1-subdivision of H in G.

    We give an expected-linear-time (for constant H) analogue of this result for some specific choices of δ, ε, η in
Section 6. The three outcome types induce different behaviors for the adversary: The first kind of outcome will be
sufficient for the adversary to choose a new wide connector and use it to generate a corresponding loss function;
the second kind of outcome would be needed in an interative manner (which may instead terminate early with
either the first or third kind of outcome), but would be eventually sufficient for the learner to produce a small
separator, and the third kind of outcome of course gives an induced minor model in G.
    For the first type of outcome, a loss function for the learning game is generated using a proxy objective Gµ,ι (p t ),
which itself is defined using a new load vector p t and the prefix sum of previous load vectors q t . These vectors,
unlike λ t , are of linear size, and are thus much easier to work with computationally. Our particular choice of Gµ,ι
also gives us a direct relationship between ∇Gµ,ι (λ) and the vertex-weighted graph radius. These are almost the
weights induced above, except that we also require a regularization term as part of the FTRL framework, and
some appropriate scaling. The scaling happens to correspond to the idea of a learning rate as defined in various
optimization methods, as well as the diameter of our metric.
    Note that the boundary between the learner and adversary (e.g., which one of them computes the vertex
weights or the prefix sum) becomes fuzzy within the efficient implementation of the algorithm. In fact, in the
actual algorithm we implement, the weights λ t are not computed explicitly at all.


2     Preliminaries
We use mostly standard graph terminology. Given a graph H and a graph G, a minor model of H in G is a collection
K of vertex-disjoint connected subgraphs of G such that:



                                                            7
    • [Vertex correspondence]: there is a one-to-one correspondence between subgraphs of K and vertices of H.
      This means K has exactly |V (H)| subgraphs.
    • [Edge correspondence]: For every edge (u, v) ∈ E(H), there is an edge in G between the two corresponding
      subgraphs Cu and C v in K.

   G is H-minor-free if it does not contain a minor model for H.
   We say that K is an induced minor model of H in G if, in addition to satisfying the vertex correspondence and
edge correspondence properties above, it satisfies:

    • [Non-edge correspondence]: If there is no edge between two vertices u and v of H, then there is no edge
      in G between the two corresponding subgraphs Cu and C v in K.

    G is H-induced-minor-free if it does not contain an induced minor model for H. Because of the non-edge
correspondence property, an H-induced-minor-free graph can be dense. For example, the clique Kn excludes P2 (a
                                                                                              (1)
path of length 2) as an induced minor. Every graph H with |V (H)| = h is an induced minor of Kh , so if G does
                                                                  (1)
not contain H as an induced minor, then it does not contain Kh as an induced minor either. Thus, in some parts
                                     (1)
of our paper, we work directly with Kh -induced-minor-free graphs.
    We also use the following single-source shortest path algorithm on graphs with integer weights by Dial [Dia69].

Theorem 5 (Dial’s algorithm [Dia69]). Let G = (V, E, w) be a graph with integer weights on the edges. Let D be
the diameter of G, n = |V |, and m = |E|. Then computing the single-source shortest path from any source vertex
takes O(n + m + D) time.
