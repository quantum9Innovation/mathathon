PROBLEM 21 / READING GUIDE

Why centering helps a random matching
A permutation matrix records a random one-to-one matching. Every row and every column
contains exactly one entry equal to one. All other entries are zero.
Subtracting the mean 1/N from an entry is called centering. The question is what happens when
we multiply many such entries and take the expectation. This is the small probabilistic calculation
hidden inside much larger spectral-gap proofs.

For cells sharing a row, both indicators cannot equal one. Their centered product instead has
expectation −1/N 2 .
The lesson. Separate cells gain an extra factor of about 1/N . Conflicting cells do not. Taking
absolute values before evaluating the product would hide this cancellation.
What the manuscript adds. A direct proof handles arbitrary mixed products, including
repeated cells and independent matching colors. It gives an exact attained maximum when there
are zero or one conflicting centered singletons. For distinct diagonal cells, we determine the first
transition, a hierarchy of later corrections, and the sharp first error. One formula covers every
permitted order.
The known starting recurrence and leading terms are credited to Collins and Nagatsu. The graph
application is the estimate in Bordenave’s Proposition 28, imported by Brito, Dumitriu and Harris.

THE PROOF IN THREE MOVES

Preserve cancellation until it has paid off
Call a requested cell isolated if no other requested cell of the same color shares its row or column.
An isolated centered singleton appears once, centered, and with no uncentered copy of that cell.
1. Set those singletons aside. Every other cell factor is an affine expression c + dM , because
an indicator satisfies M 2 = M . Expanding these factors selects some matches that must occur.
2. Condition only on compatible matches. If r selected matches have distinct rows and
columns, they occur with probability 1/(N )r . Delete their rows and columns. The remainder is
exactly a uniform permutation of size N − r.
The original centering is still 1/N . For the remaining matrix U , set Z = N U − 1. Every remaining
row now sums to r. It does not sum to zero.
3. Sum a row before taking absolute values. Let µj be the expectation of the product of j
separated Z entries. The row sum gives

                       (N − r − j + 1)µj = (r + 2j − 2)µj−1 + (j − 1)µj−2 .

For j = 1, omit the last term. The initial values are µ0 = 1 and µ1 = r/(N − r) for r < N . Full
depletion uses only the empty product µ0 . All coefficients are nonnegative.
A new column keeps all j factors. A used column collides with an old factor, and the identity

                                           Zu Zv = −Zu − Zv − 1

removes one or two factors. This turns a cancellation problem into a positive recurrence we can
bound by induction.

THE SHARP MIXED BOUND

Sharp bounds with explicit attaining patterns
Fix the dimension N , the number a ≤ N of distinct requested cells, and the number h of isolated
centered singletons. Allow arbitrary repeated powers and any number of independent permutation
colors.
When there is no conflicting centered singleton, the maximum is attained by one
simple pattern: choose a cells in distinct rows and columns of a single permutation, center h of
them, and leave the other r = a − h raw.
The exact largest absolute expectation is

The polynomial has positive coefficients. Its first values are

                   P0 (N, r) = 1,      P1 (N, r) = r,     P2 (N, r) = r2 + r + N.

In general, partition the h labeled centered factors into blocks. A block of size j carries weight
((j − 1)N + r)(j − 1)!. Add the products of these weights over all partitions. This is a finite recipe
for Ph .
Why this is the maximum. Compatible conditioning produces exactly this polynomial divided
by a falling factorial. Its block weights increase when more matches are prescribed. Two separate
colors can be merged into one larger positive partition sum. The affine weights of repeated cells
are at most one. Each step gives an upper bound, and the compatible pattern above makes the
final bound an equality.

BACK TO GRAPHS

What this proves for the spectral argument
Random lifts. Each base edge carries an independent random permutation. Orient the base
edges once, and treat each permutation as a color. The mixed-product theorem directly supplies
Bordenave’s Proposition 28.
Biregular graphs. Match the left half-edges to the right half-edges. There are

                                           L = nL dL = nR dR

half-edges on each side, so the random block is a permutation matrix of size L. Its entries are
centered at 1/L. This is the input used by Brito, Dumitriu and Harris in preprint Lemma 7,
published as Lemma 5.6.
The proof chain.

   1. Expand powers of a non-backtracking matrix into walks that do not immediately reverse an
      edge.

   2. Expand a high norm moment into products of matching entries.

   3. Apply the mixed-product bound to each fixed pattern.

   4. Count the patterns and control the contribution of local tangles.

   5. Translate the non-backtracking bound back to adjacency eigenvalues.

The manuscript proves the probability estimate in step 3 and explains the surrounding chain. The
graph enumeration and tangle remainder are separate requirements.
A concrete spectral bridge. If the degrees are dL , dR ≥ 2, put α = dL − 1 and γ = dR − 1. A
positive adjacency singular value s and the associated non-backtracking parameter satisfy
                                             αγ
                                  s2 = x +      + α + γ,       x = z2.

An s above the upper endpoint forces a non-backtracking root outside the circle. This is why
controlling those roots yields a spectral gap.
What remains separate. The sharper moment formulas do not automatically give a better
graph eigenvalue bound. The proof does not certify every later step in the cited graph papers.
Classical asymptotic methods provide another route to the diagonal corrections. The attained
mixed maximum is a distinct contribution candidate, with historical priority still subject to a
bounded literature assessment.
