Statement
---------
Fix 0<alpha<1. For every integer n>=2 set
 lambda_k=4 sin^2(k*pi/(2n)), 1<=k<=n-1,
 (H_n^alpha)_(ij)=n^-1 K_alpha(log lambda_i-log lambda_j),
 K_alpha(s)=sinh(alpha*s/2)/sinh(s/2), K_alpha(0)=alpha.
Then

 limsup_(epsilon->0) sup_(n>=2)
       N_(H_n^alpha)(epsilon)/log^2(1/epsilon) <=1/(2*pi^2).

In fact a single fixed continuum Cauchy operator bounds the low-node
counts of EVERY finite matrix, while its high nodes contribute only
O_alpha(log(1/epsilon)) uniformly in n. No fixed-n convergence or Hilbert-
Schmidt limit is used to infer this uniform assertion.

1. Fourier domination reduces every fixed alpha to alpha=1/2
------------------------------------------------------------
With Fourier transform integral e^(-i xi s)K_alpha(s) ds, the exact formula is

 sigma_alpha(xi)=2*pi*sin(pi*alpha)/
                         [cosh(2*pi*xi)+cos(pi*alpha)] >0.

For example substitute z=e^s and use the difference of the two convergent
principal-value Euler integrals with exponents
p=(1+alpha)/2-i*xi and q=(1-alpha)/2-i*xi. This gives
pi[cot(pi*q)-cot(pi*p)], which simplifies to the displayed expression.
The difference has no singularity at z=1, and both exponents have real
parts in (0,1), so these manipulations are valid. At alpha=1/2 this is
2*pi/cosh(2*pi*xi).

Their ratio is
 sin(pi*alpha) cosh(2*pi*xi)/
                         [cosh(2*pi*xi)+cos(pi*alpha)],
which is bounded by C_alpha=max(1,tan(pi*alpha/2)) for every real xi.
Fourier inversion therefore proves the positive-definite kernel order

 [K_alpha(s_i-s_j)] <= C_alpha [K_(1/2)(s_i-s_j)]

on EVERY finite real grid. Thus H_n^alpha<=C_alpha H_n^(1/2) in Loewner
order for all n, with no quadrature error. A constant threshold scaling
does not change a logarithmic-squared leading count. It suffices to treat
alpha=1/2 below.

2. Low nodes have disjoint equal-width left cells
--------------------------------------------------
Put x_k=sqrt(lambda_k)=2 sin(k*pi/(2n)), h=1/n, and
I_low={1,...,floor(n/2)}. Then

 K_(1/2)(log lambda_i-log lambda_j)=sqrt(x_i*x_j)/(x_i+x_j).

Every low node is <=sqrt(2). Also x_1>=2/n=2h, by the elementary
sin t>=2t/pi on [0,pi/2]. For consecutive low nodes, the mean-value
formula gives
 x_(k+1)-x_k >=pi/(sqrt(2)*n)>2/n.
Consequently the intervals J_k=[x_k-h,x_k] are disjoint, positive,
and contained in (0,sqrt(2)). These facts hold for all n>=2;
no large-n restriction is necessary.

Let S be the fixed positive integral operator on L2(0,sqrt(2)) with
kernel
                         S(x,y)=sqrt(x*y)/(x+y).

It is Hilbert--Schmidt, and positive because 1/(x+y) is a Laplace Gram
kernel. Define
 f_k(x)=sqrt(x_k)/(sqrt(h)*sqrt(x)) *1_(J_k)(x).

The f_k are orthogonal and satisfy
 ||f_k||^2=(x_k/h)log[x_k/(x_k-h)]<=2 log2=:M.
Indeed r log[r/(r-1)] decreases from 2log2 to one for r>=2.
Their map F:e_k->f_k therefore has ||F||^2<=M.

3. The exact matrix inequality, with its correct direction
------------------------------------------------------------
Let B_ij=<f_i,S f_j>. Cancelling the square roots in its double integral
and applying 1/(x+y)=integral_0^infinity e^(-t(x+y)) dt gives

 B_ij=h sqrt(x_i*x_j) integral_0^infinity
             e^(-t(x_i+x_j)) phi(h*t)^2 dt,
 phi(z)=(e^z-1)/z, phi(0)=1.

The equal cell width h is essential: the SAME scalar phi(h*t) occurs
for all i and j. Since phi(z)>=1 for z>=0,

 B-H_low
 =h integral_0^infinity [phi(h*t)^2-1] v(t)v(t)^* dt >=0,
 v_i(t)=sqrt(x_i)e^(-t*x_i),

where H_low is the low principal block of H_n^(1/2). The integrals
converge because x_i>=2h; there is no endpoint divergence hidden by
subtracting these positive quantities. This is a Loewner domination,
not merely entrywise comparison or an approximate quadrature rule.

Since B=F* S F, the variational/singular-value inequality yields
 lambda_j(B)<=||F||^2 lambda_j(S)<=M lambda_j(S).
One can also normalize the disjoint f_k to an isometry, apply compression
interlacing, and then bound the diagonal normalization by M.
Therefore, uniformly over n and every threshold t>0,

                    N_(H_low)(t)<=N_S(t/M).                (A)

4. High nodes have a uniformly exponential finite-rank approximation
---------------------------------------------------------------------
For k>floor(n/2), x_k lies in [c,2], c=sqrt(2). On this entire fixed
rectangle, expand in x around c:

 1/(x+y)=sum_(l=0)^(r-1) (-1)^l (x-c)^l/(c+y)^(l+1)
                  +(-1)^r (x-c)^r/[(c+y)^r(x+y)].

The expansion ratio is at most
 theta=(2-c)/(2c)<1.
After multiplication by sqrt(x*y), the kernel remainder is bounded
in absolute value by theta^r/c. The first r terms give a matrix of
rank at most r. Multiplying by h and summing rows/columns bounds its
operator-norm error by at most
 h*(number of high nodes)*theta^r/c<=theta^r/c.
The approximant need not be symmetric: the spectral-norm best rank-r
inequality still bounds the (r+1)-st singular value, and the high block
itself is PSD. Hence, for a numerical constant C,

             N_(H_high)(t)<=C[1+log_+(1/t)]                (B)

for ALL n. If the number of high nodes is smaller than r, the statement
is immediate. This argument also handles the crowded nodes near x=2;
there is no mesh regularity assumption on the high block.

5. Combining the blocks without losing the leading constant
------------------------------------------------------------
One must not discard the off-diagonal low/high blocks of H. Instead
use its exact Laplace feature representation. If v_k(t)=sqrt(x_k)e^(-x_k t)
in L2(0,infinity), then H=h V*V. Its nonzero eigenvalues equal those of
h V V*=T_low+T_high, the sum of the two positive feature operators.
These feature operators have the same nonzero spectra as H_low,H_high.
Ky Fan's positive sum inequality gives

 N_(H_n^(1/2))(epsilon)
       <=N_(H_low)(epsilon/2)+N_(H_high)(epsilon/2)
       <=N_S(epsilon/(2M))+C[1+log_+(1/epsilon)].           (C)

This exact comparison is uniform in n. Combining with Fourier domination,

 N_(H_n^alpha)(epsilon)
 <=N_S(epsilon/(2M*C_alpha))
                  +C[1+log_+(C_alpha/epsilon)]             (D)

for every n>=2. In particular it controls possible finite-quadrature
overshoot; it does not assume those eigenvalues approach the continuum
monotonically.

6. The continuum constant is a classical input, correctly specialized
----------------------------------------------------------------------
Laptev's theorem on the kernel x^beta*y^gamma/(x+y)^a on (0,1) assumes
a>0, beta,gamma>-1/2 and rho=beta+gamma-a+1>0, and gives singular-count
coefficient 1/(2*pi^2*rho) at logarithmic-squared scale. For a=1 and
beta=gamma=1/2, rho=1 and the operator is positive, so this is precisely
an eigenvalue count. Rescaling the interval from (0,1) to (0,sqrt(2))
only multiplies the operator by sqrt(2), which shifts log(1/epsilon)
by a constant. Consequently

                  N_S(epsilon)~log^2(1/epsilon)/(2*pi^2).

The primary theorem and parameter conditions were freshly inspected on
page 1039 (PDF page 2) of A. A. Laptev, Spectral asymptotic behavior of a
class of integral operators, Mathematical Notes 16 (1974), 1038--1043.
Author-hosted primary PDF:
 https://www.ma.imperial.ac.uk/~alaptev/Papers/MatZam74.pdf
Publisher/MathNet record confirms the exact pi-squared constant:
 https://www.mathnet.ru/php/archive.phtml?jrnid=mzm&option_lang=eng&paperid=7513&wshow=paper
The theorem is classical; no claim of a new continuum asymptotic is made.

Now divide (D) by log^2(1/epsilon), take the supremum over ALL n first,
and then epsilon->0. Its O(log(1/epsilon)) remainder vanishes and fixed
threshold factors do not change the Laptev coefficient. This proves
the requested limsup upper bound with exactly 1/(2*pi^2).

7. Consequence for paths, conditional only on the supplied derivative bound
----------------------------------------------------------------------------
For the path response, the given positive derivative bound is
 C_path<=D H_n^alpha D,
 D_kk=sqrt(2) sin(p*k*pi/n), ||D||<=sqrt(2).
Congruence and singular-value min--max give
lambda_j(D H_n^alpha D)<=2 lambda_j(H_n^alpha), even when D has zero
or negative diagonal entries. Therefore the same upper leading coefficient
holds uniformly over all path lengths and all edited locations p.
This step uses the explicitly supplied derivative bound; the uniform
arcsine matrix result above does not depend on assuming monotonicity of
the actual centered path-response spectra.

The cycle analogue can be treated by the same cells with h=2/n after
