                                         Abstract
    Bordenave’s Proposition 28 controls centered permutation products in random-lift and
biregular spectral-gap proofs. We first give an intuitive proof of this established estimate:
compatible conditioning leaves a smaller uniform permutation matrix, whose exact row
sum exposes the cancellation. The argument handles arbitrary powers and independent
colors, and gives attained envelopes with zero, one or two conflicting centered singletons.
The underlying recurrence and classical counting inputs are credited.
    Our main new contribution is exact recovery of an unknown bounded-size degree
frame and its hidden classes in sparse block-regular graphs. Under a symmetric omission
certificate, independent uniform simple factors may have unequal class sizes, varying
degrees, and absent internal edges. With high probability, the hidden partition is the
unique finest equitable partition with the supplied fixed class bound. A certified complete-
listing algorithm uses unconditional expected nearly quadratic bit work and expected
nearly linear working space, without a planted eigengap or real-corank-one promise.
Every fixed connected simple regular template of degree at least three is covered. The
main text includes the complete rigidity, raw finite-field moment, and algorithmic proofs.
Supplementary appendices retain the quantitative diagonal-moment refinements. These
are exact-count, fixed-parameter results; noise stability, practical speed, and historical
firstness are not asserted.

1     Introduction and prior work
1.1   The original question: a proof of Proposition 28
Bordenave’s random-lift proof of Friedman’s second-eigenvalue theorem uses a bound on
products of centered permutation entries [1, Proposition 28]. The same probabilistic input
appears in Bordenave–Collins [5, Proposition 22] and in the biregular spectral-gap argument
of Brito–Dumitriu–Harris [2, Lemma 5.6]. The estimate and the graph eigenvalue theorems
are established prior work. Problem 21 asks for an intuitive proof of that estimate and an
explanation of its spectral role. Sections 2 and 3 give that answer before the new recovery
results.
    A permutation matrix has exactly one nonzero entry in each row and column. Its entries
have mean 1/N , but they are dependent. For a uniform permutation matrix M , two distinct
diagonal cells satisfy
                                                    1      1     1
              E[(M11 − N −1 )(M22 − N −1 )] =            − 2 = 2        .                  (1)
                                                N (N − 1) N   N (N − 1)

Two cells in one row instead have centered expectation −N −2 . The smaller diagonal scale
is cancellation, not independence. Taking absolute values before using the row and column
constraints would lose it.
    Our proof keeps isolated centered singletons together, conditions only on a compatible
set of prescribed matches, and sums a row of the remaining smaller uniform permutation
matrix. The resulting positive recurrence permits the remaining factors to be expanded
safely. Theorem 2.1 gives the exact mixed-product envelope; Corollary 2.8 recovers the
requested scalar estimate. Theorems 2.5 and 2.6 give attained maxima with one or two
conflicting singletons. The base recurrence, parity-dependent leading terms, and normalized
error recurrences are due to Collins–Nagatsu [3, Proposition 3.3]; we credit and reuse them.
The graph path enumeration and tangle estimates remain cited inputs, rather than new
graph-norm theorems proved here.

1.2   Prior work on recovering hidden graph groups
A separate line of work asks whether exact neighbor-count groups can be found after their
labels are erased. Algebraic fiber recovery is not a new idea: Fellows–Koblitz discussed hidden
fibers in K4 covers [21], and Bernstein–Cottaar–Lange used echelon reduction and grouping
of affine expressions to expose those fibers [22]. Regular-block-model rigidity and spectral
recovery also have direct predecessors [26, 27, 28, 2]. Our result should be compared with
these recovery results, rather than with noiseless detection alone.
    The counting tools likewise have established sources. Vontobel’s cover enumerators and
entropy interpretation [23], Mori–Tanaka’s positive interior prefactors [24], and Mészáros’s
finite-group mixing [25] are relevant predecessors. The sparse linear algebra uses classical
recurrence and preconditioning methods [18, 19]. We do not claim to invent Gaussian elimi-
nation, cover entropy, finite-field moments, or black-box linear algebra. Further comparisons
for the original moment refinements appear in Appendix H.

Reading and proof route. First read Section 2 for the complete Proposition 28 input
and Section 3 for its spectral interpretation. Section 4 then states the main recovery theorem

refinements and their proofs; they are supplementary to the main recovery result.

Scope of the claims. All recovery parameters are fixed as the graph grows, the sampling
laws are specified and independent, and neighbor counts are exact. The listing guarantee
is always-correct; choosing the hidden plant also uses the stated probabilistic structural
event. Expected nearly quadratic work is not a worst-case bound or a benchmark claim.
Neither the recovery theorem nor the moment estimates establish robustness to noisy edges,
growing-parameter uniformity, or a new second-eigenvalue bound. Written proofs and internal
checks support the stated claims; external peer review and historical firstness are not certified.

3.3   Why isolated centered factors help a trace expansion

graph, it reduces to v = x and y ̸= u for consecutive edges u → v and x → y. Thus powers of
the matrix count walks that never immediately reverse the same edge.

4.4   Significance, predecessors, and limits
The contribution is the combination of unknown count tables, a bounded number of groups,
all admissible sizes, sparse independent factors, complete certification, and an unconditional
expected-work guarantee. It separates three questions: whether the hidden groups are usually
identifiable, whether every valid answer can be listed correctly, and whether the complete
computation has a proved average cost.
    Algebraic fiber recovery has direct predecessors. Fellows–Koblitz’s Kid Krypto work
discussed the four hidden fibers of K4 covers [21]; Bernstein–Cottaar–Lange later used echelon
reduction and grouping of affine expressions to expose such fibers [22]. We do not claim
to originate Gaussian recovery. Graph-cover counting and entropy [23], positive interior
prefactors [24], and finite-group mixing [25] likewise have established predecessors. The exact
scope and composition proved here should be assessed against that literature; this report
does not certify historical firstness.
    All parameters are fixed as the graph grows, the factor choices have the stated independent
uniform laws, and neighbor counts are exact. Missing or added edges need not preserve the
equations. No noise-stability, growing-parameter, arbitrary-network clustering, numerical-
eigengap, or practical-speed theorem follows. The always-correct guarantee concerns listing;
identifying the particular plant additionally uses the high- probability structural theorem.
Finite checks and internal mathematical reviews supplement the written proofs and do not
constitute formal verification or external peer review.

H     Prior work and limits of the moment refinements
The mixed-product theorems identify attained maxima when there are zero, one or two
inconsistent centered singletons. Their proofs keep the exact depleted moment, merge colors
through positive partition weights, and handle arbitrary multiplicities through affine indicator
identities. For one distinguished inconsistent singleton, an exact kernel difference exchanges
a missing conflicting neighbor for one extra prescribed match. This also improves the general
inconsistent-singleton bound. The scalar consequences recover the spectral estimate with a
smaller singleton factor and an optimal universal prefactor. The diagonal results determine
the original cubic threshold, the parity transition, a hierarchy of further corrections and sharp
first uniform errors for both the square-root and full-range approximations.

cubic scale. Classical uniform asymptotic theory supplies another route to the corrections.

for z > 0, from negative infinity to positive infinity. It has exactly one positive zero. The
function g increases before this zero and decreases afterward, proving (119) and uniqueness.
This completes the proof of Theorem E.4.
