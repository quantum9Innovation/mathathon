                                                       Let λk (G) be the kth eigenvalue of the normalized Laplacian of a finite undirected weighted
                                                    graph G with positive degrees, where k is an integer satisfying 1 ≤ k ≤ |V (G)|. Let φk (G) be
                                                    the minimum possible maximum conductance of k disjoint nonempty vertex sets. We prove
                                                                                                p
                                                                                    φk (G) ≤ C λk (G) log(k + 1)

                                                    conductance is measured in the original graph. The logarithmic dependence is optimal up to an
                                                    absolute constant. The proof combines geometric partitioning of the spectral embedding with
                                                    minimum-cut improvement and adaptive projections in coefficient space. A single conductance

1.1   Proof overview
Let F : Rk → L2 (d) have as columns eigenfunctions for the first k eigenvalues of the random-walk
Laplacian, orthonormal in the degree-weighted inner product. Every nonzero function in its range
has Rayleigh quotient at most λk .
                                                                       p
A fixed conductance threshold. We use one threshold τ = C0 λk log(k + 1) throughout,
where C0 is a sufficiently large absolute constant. For a vertex set B, choose S ⊆ B to minimize
                                   cut(T ) − τ vol(T ),    T ⊆ B.
If S is nonempty, then φ(S) ≤ τ . Comparison with S \ T also shows that every T ⊆ S satisfies
                                w(T, S c ) ≤ τ vol(T ) + w(T, S \ T ).                           (1.4)
This is the boundary inequality used in Lau’s local-to-global lemma for cores [2, Lemma 7.18],
with τ in place of φ(S). For nonempty T , it implies φ(T ) ≤ τ + 2φS (T ), where φS (T ) is local
conductance with original degrees in the denominator. The same minimization also controls how
much spectral mass is rejected.

                                                   2
Keeping the remaining directions. At each stage, we work in a subspace of coefficient space.
Geometric partitioning and cut improvement select disjoint sets from the unassigned vertices.
Vertices that are not selected remain available. We then retain the spectral subspace of the
remaining Gram matrix corresponding to eigenvalues close to one. The corresponding spectral
functions have little mass on previously selected sets. The inequality (1.4) then controls the
boundary contributions from those sets without increasing τ .
    A trace estimate suffices to reduce the dimension of this subspace by a constant factor. Because
selection is by vertex sets, the Gram matrices on the selected and remaining vertices add exactly.
The discarded coefficient spaces are orthogonal, so after O(log k) stages the sum of the Gram
matrices of the selected maps is bounded below by an absolute positive multiple of the identity.
Each individual Gram matrix has a small sum of eigenvalues other than the largest.

Obtaining exactly k sets. The selected maps use different, nested coefficient projections. A
dyadic maximal estimate bounds the sum of their internal energies, uniformly over unit coefficient
vectors, by O((1 + log log(k + 2))2 λk ). A dimension argument then shows that the local eigenvalue
counts below a constant multiple of this bound, each truncated at two, sum to at least k. Local
Cheeger rounding gives one or two sets from each selected vertex set. The boundary inequality
converts their local conductance to original conductance at cost
                                                               p
                                  τ + O((1 + log log(k + 2)) λk ),
            p
which is O( λk log(k + 1)).
    The proof follows this order. Section 2 fixes the local operators and spectral notation. Sections 3
and 4 establish cut improvement and geometric partitioning. Section 5 constructs the selected
maps, and Section 6 completes the proof.
