                                           1        Introduction
                                           The separator theorem for planar graphs by Lipton and Tarjan [LT79] in 1979 is a cornerstone of algorithmic graph
                                           theory. Given a graph G = (V, E), a vertex subset S ⊂ V is a balanced separator if every connected component of
                                           G \S has size at most 3|V |/4. The Lipton-Tarjan theorem says that any n-vertex planar graph has a balanced (vertex)
                                                                 p                                                                             p    p
                                           separator of size O( n). This bound is asymptotically optimal: any balanced separator of the n × n-planar
                                                                   p
                                           grid must have size Ω( n). A similar result was soon shown for bounded genus graphs [GHT84], which is also
                                           asymptotically tight. A decade later, Alon, Seymour, and Thomas [AST90] (AST) generalized the planar separator
                                           theorem to Kh -minor-free graphs. They showed that any n-vertex Kh -minor-free graph has a balanced separator of
                                                       p                                                                    p
                                           size O(h3/2 n). In particular, for a constant h, the AST separator has size O( n); this applies to planar graphs,
                                           which are K5 -minor-free. In the regime of constant h ⩾ 5, their separator bound is also asymptotically optimal,
                                                              p    p
                                           witnessed by the n × n-planar grid.
                                               For non-constant h, Alon, Seymour, and Thomas [AST90] conjectured that the bound is not optimal. They
                                                                                                                                               p
                                           pointed out that for 3-regular expander graphs with n vertices,ph = Θ(n1/2 ), but the bound O(h3/2 n) = O(n1/4 n),
                                           while a (trivial) balanced separator has size n. This excess of h is their basis for the following conjecture.

                                           Conjecture 1 (Alon-Seymour-Thomas Conjecture [AST90]). Any Kh -minor-free graph with n vertices has a
                                                                                p
                                           balanced vertex separator of size O(h n) for any h ⩾ 3.

                                                                                                        p
Theorem 1. Any n-vertex Kh -minor-free graph G has a 3/4-balanced separator of size at most 16h n.

      In Section 1.2, we give a detailed overview of the proof. Here we highlight two new ideas underlying the proof.

The first idea is a bound on the length of a walk intersecting k large subsets of vertices in a vertex expander.
To formally state the bound, we recall the definition of a vertex expander. Given a connected graph G = (V, E)
and a subset S ⊆ V of vertices, we define the (outer) boundary ∂ S := {v ∈ V \ S : ∃u ∈ S, uv ∈ E}, which is the
set of vertices in V \ S that have at least one neighbor in S. We say that G is a ψ-vertex expander for a parameter
ψ > 0 if, for every S ⊆ V such that |S| ⩽ |V |/2, |∂ S| ⩾ ψ|S|. That is, every vertex subset of size at most |V |/2 has
a sufficiently large external neighbor set in G. We denote by ψ(G) = max{ψ : G is a ψ-vertex expander}.
    The central new ingredient of the proof of Theorem 1 is the following theorem, which gives a short walk that
intersects every set in a given collection of large subsets. Note that these subsets may not be vertex-disjoint.

Theorem 2 (Short Hitting Walk on Expanders). Let G = (V, E) be a connected graph on n vertices. Let X 1 , . . . , X k ⊂
V be k subsets of vertices, each of size at least n/k. Then G has a walk W of length less than 13k/ψ(G) that
intersects every subset: W ∩ X i ̸= ∅ for every i ∈ [k].

      To place Theorem 2 in context, we compare it with the following lemma of Alon, Seymour, and Thomas [AST90].
Lemma 1 (Lemma 2.1 [AST90]). Let G = (V, E) be an n-vertex graph and ℓ > 0 be a parameter. Let X 1 , . . . , X k
be k subsets of vertices of G. Then there exists either:
   1. a tree T in G of at most ℓk vertices such that T ∩ X i ̸= ∅ for every i ∈ [k], or
   2. a subset Z ⊆ V of size |Z| < n/ℓ such that for every connected component C of G \ Z, C ∩ X i = ; for some

    Alon, Seymour, and Thomas [AST90] observed that improving the trade-off between the tree size hℓ and the
                                                                                     p
separator size n/ℓ would improve their balanced-separator bound (which is O(h3/2 n)). However, Kotlov [Kot00]
later showed that the tradeoff is tight when ℓ is constant, and G is the hypercube. Subsequent papers either avoid
the AST framework entirely [KR10, BLR10, Spa25] or exploit Kh -minor-freeness to get a better tradeoff [Le26].
(Herein, for simplicity, we assume that k = h since this is the most interesting regime.) Specifically, Le [Le26] uses
the optimal padded decomposition for Kh -minor-free graphs [CF25] to construct a tree T of size O(ℓ log h) (in a
very specific setting), improving over the bound O(hℓ) in Lemma 1. The log h factor is essentially the padding
parameter, which is best possible as noted above.
    The walk-length bound in Theorem 2 improves the bound on |V (T )| in Lemma 1 to O(ℓ) when ℓ = c · h/ψ(G)
for a sufficiently large constant c. To see this, assume |X i | = n/h for every i ∈ [k], and let Z ⊆ V be any
set with |Z| < n/ℓ = ψ(G) · n/(c · h). Every union S of components of G \ Z with |S| ⩽ n/2 has ∂ S ⊆ Z, so
|S| ⩽ |Z|/ψ(G) < n/(c · h) by the definition of a vertex expander. Take such an S of maximum size. By maximality,
every other component C of G \ Z has |C| > n/2 − |S| > n/2 − n/(c · h), which is greater than n/(c · h) > |S| for
c ⩾ 2, and therefore |C| > n/2. So G \ Z consists of S and a single component C, which intersects every X i because
|V \ C| = |S| + |Z| < n/(c · h) + ψ(G) · n/(c · h) = (1 + ψ(G)) · n/(c · h) ⩽ n/h = |X i | for any c ⩾ 3 ⩾ 1 + ψ(G).
Hence Z does not satisfy case 2 of Lemma 1, which requires every component of G \ Z to miss some X i . Hence,
case 2 cannot occur, and case 1 gives a tree T with at most
                                                           hℓ = O(h2 /ψ(G))
vertices that intersects every X i . However, Theorem 2 gives a tree — more precisely, a spanning tree of the subgraph
G[V (W )] where W is the walk — of only O(h/ψ(G)) vertices that intersects every X i , a factor of h improvement!
Note that Theorem 2 does not exploit Kh -minor-freeness, and yet still gives a factor of h improvement over
Lemma 1.
    At this point, readers might ask: does Theorem 2 contradict Kotlov’s [Kot00] lower bound, which shows the
tightness of Lemma 1? One observation is that Theorem 2 requires |X i | ⩾ n/k, whereas Lemma 1 imposes no such
requirement. However, the lower bound instance of Kotlov [Kot00] has |X i | = n/2, so assuming large subsets is not
the source of the improvement. The key fact here is that the parameter setting ℓ = c · k/ψ(G) in the application of
Theorem 2 is outside the lower bound regime of Kotlov. Specifically, Kotlov’s lower bound applies p  to the case ℓ = 2,
k ≈ 2 log(n) and G is the (discrete) hypercube (in {0, 1}log n ). For the hypercube3 , ψ(G) = Θ(1/ log n) [Har66],
and hence our setting of ℓ in Theorem 2 is ℓ = c · k/ψ(G) = Θ(log3/2 (n)), which is much larger than 2. In summary,
there is no contradiction.

The second idea is to adapt the algorithm by Le [Le26], which itself is a combination of two different algo-
rithms [AST90, PRS94], so that Theorem 2 can be applied. For readers who are familiar with Le [Le26] (as well
as [AST90, PRS94]), in a nutshell, the algorithm is iterative, and every iteration essentially has two major steps:
it finds either (i) a small set of vertices Z of the current subgraph H ⊆ G that cuts off a large subset A (relative
to |Z|), or (ii) a tree T ⊆ H of small size that intersects every neighborhood of the current branch sets in the
clique minor model. Here in this paper, both steps are reframed in terms of vertex expanders. Specifically, case
(i) can be implemented as cutting off the smaller side of a sparse cut if H is not a vertex expander, while in the
(complementary) case (ii), H is a vertex expander. In this case, we split into two smaller cases: one of which
extends the neighborhood of each branch set, so its size is at least n/h, then applies Theorem 2 to find the tree T .
The other smaller case is handled similarly to prior work [AST90, Le26].

1.1       Related work
An orthogonal line of work on balanced separators of Kh -minor-free graphs focuses on fast, ideally linear-time
algorithms [AST90, PRS94, RW09, KR10, Wul11, Wul14, BKL+ 26]. Here, we assume that the minor size h is a
fixed constant. In this regime, Bonnet, Korhonen, Le, Li, and Masařík [BKL+ 26] gave a linear-time algorithm,
which concludes decades of effort on the running-time question. The dependency on h in these works could be
exponential [RW09] or polynomial with a high degree [BKL+ 26].
    Another relevant line of work focuses on constructing balanced separators for more general graph classes, such
as graphs with polynomial expansion [PRS94, DN16, ER18], H-induced-minor-free graphs [KL24], or fat-minor-
free graphs [BLPP26]. In these papers, the separator size, especially its dependency on the minor size (in their
respective definitions of graph minors), is not optimal. It would be interesting to see if the short hitting walk in
Theorem 2 can be applied to improve the separators in these more general settings.

The existence of a short hitting walk in Theorem 2. It is well known that a ψ(G)-vertex-expander has diameter
O(log n/ψ(G)), which is also tight. Thus, we can construct a walk in Theorem 2 of length O(k log n/ψ(G)) simply
by picking one vertex per subset and connecting them into a walk using shortest paths. This bound is worse than
Theorem 2 by a factor of log n and does not exploit the fact that the subsets are large (of size at least n/k).
     Getting rid of this log n-factor is more complicated. The very high-level idea of the proof is to construct a
randomized walk W that performs exactly k large steps, using shortest-paths between chosen vertices, while
selecting exactly one of the sets X 1 , . . . , X k in each step. We design the random walk to minimize the expected length
of visiting a new subset. For simplicity of the exposition in this overview, we assume that the subsets {X 1 , . . . , X k }
are pairwise vertex disjoint, and each has size exactly r := ⌈n/k⌉.
     In this special case, the random walk maintains the following invariant: After the t-th step of the walk, we
have selected a uniformly random t-element subset I ⊆ [k], and the walk is guaranteed to hit all sets X i with i ∈ I.
Moreover, conditioned on I = J (for any fixed t-element subset J ⊆ [k]), the last vertex on the walk is distributed
uniformly at random among the vertices in the selected sets {v ∈ X i : i ∈ J}.
     How should we design the random walk to maintain this invariant? Fix some J ⊆ [k] and condition on the fact
that our selected subset is I = J. We know that the last vertex vt is uniformly distributed among {v ∈ X i : i ∈ J}.
Call this distribution pJ . To maintain the invariant, we would like to select a uniformly random index i ∈ [k] \ J
and a uniformly random vertex vt+1 ∈ X i , and then extend the walk W along a shortest path from vt to vt+1 . Let
qJ denote the distribution from which vt+1 is sampled. It is not difficult to see that if we sample vt+1 according to
qJ , we maintain the desired invariant on the random walk.

correlated with pJ . We exploit the fact that G is an expander to show5 that vt+1 can be sampled from qJ such that

Theorem 2 (Short Hitting Walk on Expanders). Let G = (V, E) be a connected graph on n vertices. Let X 1 , . . . , X k ⊂
V be k subsets of vertices, each of size at least n/k. Then G has a walk W of length less than 13k/ψ(G) that

3     The Algorithm
For a subgraph H of G and a set of vertices A of G, let ∂H A denote the set of vertices of H that are not in A but
have at least one neighbor in A. We note that A might not belong to V (H); for example, when H is a subgraph of a
larger graph G and A ⊆ V (G) that is not in H. When A ⊆ V (H), we use the notation BH (A, r) to denote the ball of
radius r around A in H, that is, {v ∈ V (H) : dH (A, v) ⩽ r}. We will use the following corollary of Theorem 2.
Corollary 1. Suppose that H is a ψ-vertex expander. Let X = {X 1 , X 2 , . . . , X k } be a collection of k vertex subsets
of V (H) such that |X i | ⩾ |V (H)|/h and k ⩽ h. Then there is a procedure SHORT TREE(H, X) that produces a tree
T ⊆ H such that:
    1. T ∩ X i ̸= ∅ for every i ∈ [k].
    2. |V (T )| ⩽ ⌈13h/ψ⌉.
Proof: If k = h, then we can apply Theorem 2 to produce a walk W , and we can then simply take T to be a
spanning tree of H[V (W )]. As W has length strictly less than 13h/ψ, the tree T has at most ⌈13h/ψ⌉ vertices.
If k < h, then we duplicate X k to get X+ = {X 1 , . . . , X k , X k+1 = X k , . . . , X h = X k } and apply Theorem 2 to H and
X+ .                                                                                                                          □
