                                                       We establish that a practical polynomial-time algorithm obtains near-optimal 23 -balanced vertex
                                                   separators for a number of graph classes, including genus-g graphs, Kh -minor-free graphs, and
                                                   geometric graphs. For many of these classes, our polynomial-time separator bounds improve on
                                                   previous work. Moreover, unlike previous work, all these separators are produced by a single
                                                   unified algorithm that has no specialization for the graph class, and this algorithm admits a scalable
                                                   implementation with good empirical results. Furthermore, our approach is robust to graphs falling
                                                   just outside these classes, making it appropriate for practical applications like road networks.
                                                       A key distinction of our approach, compared to previous work, is the disentanglement of our
                                                   algorithm and its analysis for specific graph classes. Our algorithm is based on a newly improved
                                                   form of a recent Cheeger-style inequality relating the value of a special semidefinite program to vertex
                                                   expansion. Our analysis then only has to directly bound the value of this semidefinite program for
                                                   each graph class, and constructions for these bounds need not be algorithmic. Finally, we obtain
                                                   several results of independent interest. These include an improved (existential) separator theorem for
                                                   intersection graphs of k-ply d-dimensional balls, an improved bound on the Fiedler value of genus-g
                                                   graphs, and a complete spectral proof of the planar separator theorem.

would be impractical to implement.

     Our proof involves two components. First, we use a semidefinite programming relaxation of “vertex
expansion”, a quantity closely related to vertex separators. We denote the SDP by γ(n) (G), and round
it through a new Cheeger-style inequality. Second, we analyze the performance of this algorithm for
special graph classes by giving specialized bounds on the value of γ(n) (G).
     Our analysis of this algorithm applied to special graph classes uses two different kinds of methods.
First, we show that, for planar graphs, geometric graphs, and genus-g graphs, γ(n) (G) can be bounded
geometrically via the theory of circle packings. Second, we bound γ(n) (G) in terms of some well-understood
combinatorial quantities. Both methods are generalizations/improvements of prior methods bounding
Laplacian eigenvalues of graphs. Specifically, the first method builds on and improves (and in some
places fixes) works of Spielman and Teng [ST96, ST07] and Kelner [Kel04, Kel06b, Kel06a]. The second
method builds on works of Biswal, Lee, and Rao [BLR08].

to contract a “fake” edge that is not present in G, such as an overpass vertex in a road network.

2     Technical Ideas
2.1     Bounds
We use two different mechanisms for proving bounds on γ(n) (G), and more generally γ(d) (G).
    The first is geometric: A feasible solution of γ(d) (G) is interpreted as a family of balls, and the
objective is bounded by a surface-area calculation after mapping those balls to sphere caps. This is an
adaptation of a framework used by Spielman and Teng for planar graphs and geometric graphs [ST07],
as well as Kelner for genus-g graphs [Kel06a]. This method is quite involved, and requires a careful
handling of the geometry in many cases. It has also proved quite tricky: We discovered notable errors
in the proofs of both papers that we had to fix in order to adapt their methods. Our method to fix
Kelner’s proof in particular required us to devise completely new methods that resulted in a significantly
improved bound.
    The second method more directly leverages combinatorial bounds and metric embeddings: We design
an adapter based on “non-expansive embeddings” and padded decompositions that allows us to leverage
part of a pipeline for bounds on λ2 (G) developed by Biswal, Lee, and Rao [BLR08, BLR10].
    The improved Cheeger-style inequality in Theorem 1.3 is what converts our solution to γ(n) into a
partition of the graph. Similar to the most recent improvement of this kind of Cheeger-style inequality,
the improvement lies in a new dimension-reduction method to relate γ(n) and γ(1) . In our case, we apply
padded decompositions over vertex-weighted shortest path metrics to create a “partially” non-expansive
embedding into the line. This partial property is critical to our correctness.

2.1.1    Geometric Bounds For Planar and Geometric Graphs

The geometric methods primarily leverage one key fact: For a d-dimensional unit ball, we know its area
exactly with an analytic formula.
    In order to leverage this, we first show that γ(d) (G) is equivalent to a problem that embeds G into a
spanned d-dimensional ball-intersection supergraph, at the loss of a constant factor. Then, we restrict
ourselves to the special case where these balls have their centres on the surface of a unit (d − 1)-sphere
embedded in Rd . Ultimately, for each class, we construct a feasible solution to γ(d) in the form of a circle
packing of some spanned supergraph of G with some kind of bound on its ply.
    Spielman and Teng used a very similar construction to bound λ2 (G) for planar graphs and geometric
graphs. However, they had an error for geometric graphs in dimension d > 2. Their error was with
respect to the unintuitive behaviour of higher-dimensional geometry. Fortunately, we are able to derive a
replacement step that also recovers their bound.
    We will show the following bounds with these methods:

Proposition 2.1. Let G be a planar graph with n vertices. Then γ(1) (G) ≲ γ(3) (G) ≤ 8/n.

Proposition 2.2. Let G be a spanning subgraph of the intersection graph of d-dimensional balls with
ply k. Then γ(d+1) (G) ≲ (k/n)2/d .

Corollary 2.3. Let G be a spanning subgraph of a d-dimensional k-nearest-neighbour graph with n
vertices. Then γ(d+1) (G) ≲ (k/n)2/d .

    For planar graphs, the required ball representation is a circle packing on the sphere, so the ply is
1. For k-ply ball graphs, a homeomorphism used by Spielman and Teng [ST96, ST07] maps Euclidean
balls to sphere caps and preserves ply. This is where the dimension increases by one. At this point, in
either case, an inequality is needed relating the area of the sphere caps, and the radii of the balls they

represent. There is a natural inequality for the 2-sphere embedded in R3 , but it does not extend to
higher dimensions, which is exactly the error in Spielman and Teng’s work. We replace this step with
Lemma 4.7, which is enough for the k-ply surface-area charging argument.

    For those who work with minors, this definition may appear backwards, but it is the right way around

for which G is a uniform shallow minor. We then show how to relate the values of γ(1) (G) and γ(1) (H),
along with the depth (or “shallowness”) of the minor. In particular, to do this, we randomly embed G
into H. The vertices of G get mapped to a subset of vertices of H, and the edges of G get mapped to
paths in H. We then show that the needed relationship between the values of the corresponding feasible
solutions for γ(1) holds with positive probability.
    We apply this technique with carefully constructed larger graphs in order to show both of the following
results.

4.1    Planar Graphs
Planar graphs admit a very well-structured representation as a ball-intersection graph. This comes from
the celebrated circle packing theorem:

Theorem 4.3 (Planar Circle Packing Theorem [Koe36, And70, TM79]). Let G be a simple undirected
graph. Then G is the geometric intersection graph of 2-dimensional balls with ply 1 (a circle packing) if

unique up to Möbius transformations.

hence giving a “spectral” proof of a weaker version of the planar separator theorem via the usual Cheeger
inequality for edge expansion. We will use a similar argument to bound γ(1) (G), hence also implying a
“spectral”-type of proof of the planar separator theorem in full generality via Theorem 1.3. We will make

In this subsection, we will prove Proposition 2.6, which states that a genus-g graph G with maximum
                         g log ∆

    This will involve several steps. At a high-level, we will use two different groups of techniques:
    • Techniques involving uniform shallow-minors, which we will define shortly.
    • Techniques based on the theory of circle packings for genus-g graphs, adapting constructions used
      by Kelner to give a bound on the Fiedler value λ2 for triangulated genus-g graphs [Kel04, Kel06b,
      Kel06a].
    We will now briefly outline some important definitions and challenges related to genus-g graphs.
Recall that a genus-g graph is one that can be drawn on a genus-g (oriented) surface without crossing
edges. Such a drawing is called a surface embedding, and it implies the existence of a rotation system:
A (circular) ordering of the edges around each vertex. Like a plane graph, we can define a face of a
genus-g graph as any cycle that is formed by a tour induced by always moving counter-clockwise in
the rotation system at a vertex to choose the next edge, starting from the previous. A rotation system
(and a corresponding embedding) is called cellular if each edge is a part of exactly two such tours. Not
every surface embedding is cellular, even for plane graphs: A face on a plane graph can include an edge
twice, each time traversed in opposite directions. For instance, this is always true of cut edges in any
embedding of a planar graph (see Figure 7). In a surface embedding for a genus-g graph with g ≥ 1, it
can also be the case that an edge is included twice in the same direction (see Figure 7).




Figure 7. Non-cellular embeddings: planar graph (left) and K5 in the flat torus (right). A non-cellular face in each is traced.


    For simple graphs, we call a graph embedded in a surface with cellular faces a triangulation if
all of its faces consist of exactly 3 edges. Planar triangulations correspond exactly to maximal planar
graphs, which are graphs for which the addition of any edge would enforce the invalidation of planarity,
regardless of embedding. For graphs of genus-g, this is no longer true:

Example 4.12. K5 is a maximal graph of genus 1, and K5 admits no triangulated embedding into a
surface of genus 1.

      See Figure 7 for an embedding of K5 in the torus.

Proof: The proof is by contradiction. Suppose this was not the case, and that it had a triangulated
embedding in the torus. K5 has 5 vertices and 10 edges. By the Euler characteristic, an embedding
of K5 in the torus must have 5 faces. We will now count the number of edge-face incidences in two
different ways. Since the embedding is triangulated (and hence cellular), each edge is incident to exactly
two faces, and there are 10 edges and hence 2 · 10 = 20 incidences in total. Since the embedding is
triangulated, there are exactly 3 edges incident to each face, and hence there are 3 · 5 = 15 incidences in
total, giving a contradiction.                                                                           □

                                                             19
    Kelner claimed to have bounded λ2 for all genus-g graphs, rather than just triangulations. The
method he used to try and prove this was to reduce the case of general genus-g graphs to the case of
triangulated genus-g graphs. However, the reduction used for this is actually incorrect. The claimed
argument is as follows:

   • Adding edges to a graph can only increase λ2 (this is also true for γ(1) ).
   • Add edges until the graph is maximal.
   • Apply the bound on triangulated genus-g graphs that Kelner proves in the rest of his paper. This
     bound relies on a generalized circle packing theorem of He and Schramm [HS93] that applies only
     to simple triangulations of genus-g surfaces.

This argument has two separate issues:

   • As mentioned, a maximal genus-g graph is not always a triangulation.
   • Even in a planar graph, naively adding edges may increase the maximum degree, possibly up to
     as large as Ω(n), and the bound that Kelner obtains for triangulations has a dependence on the
     maximum degree.

It is worth noting that both of these issues can be sidestepped for planar graphs: Maximal planar graphs
are always planar triangulations, and there is a known result that triangulates a planar graph while
asymptotically preserving the maximum degree [KB92, KB97]. It is possible that these issues could be
amended for genus-g graphs by allowing augmentation to obtain a multigraph, but such a construction
is not known, and it would need to be checked that Kelner’s construction can be made to work with a
multigraph. Instead, we have both generalized and improved Kelner’s bound via our other techniques.
     We now move onto the proof. This will involve several phases. Henceforth, we will fix a rotation
system for our initial graph G. This rotation system need not be cellular, but we will assume that G is
simple (as we do throughout this paper). Importantly for our purposes, so long as we carefully respect
this rotation system, we will be able to make certain local transformations to a graph without changing
its genus.

4.3.1   Uniform Shallow Minors

Definition 4.13. Let G = (V, E) be a graph with n vertices, and let H = (V ′ , E ′ ) be a graph with r · n

p(u)p(v) ∈ E. Then we say that G is a uniform shallow minor of H with depth L.

4.3.2    Circle Packings for Bounded-Genus Graphs

Because of the lemmas in Section 4.3.1 we henceforth need only consider triangulated genus-g graphs
of constant degree. We will now begin generalizing the arguments of Kelner [Kel04, Kel06b, Kel06a] to
obtain bounds on γ(1) (G) for this class.
    A genus-g graph naturally embeds into a genus-g surface. Moreover, a genus-g graph in fact has
a circle packing on a genus-g surface. Both the surface and packing is unique up to a certain set of
transformations [HS93]. However, to bound γ(3) in a similar manner to Proposition 2.1, we would need a
circle packing on the unit sphere S 2 , likely of ply O(g). Unfortunately, as Kelner [Kel04, Kel06b, Kel06a]
pointed out, such circle packings need-not exist. Instead, in order to bound λ2 , Kelner made use of two
steps: First, a specific subdivision procedure is applied to the graph, and it is shown that a bound for
the graph obtained by a sequence of subdivision procedures also induces a bound on the initial graph.
Second, by applying this procedure a sufficient number of times, it is shown that a continuous analogue
of the circle packing theory can be used to obtain a bound. We will apply analogues of each of these
steps for γ(3) (and γ(1) , which differs only by a constant factor of at most 3) instead of λ2 .

7.1    Algorithmic Details
Many pieces of the rounding algorithm are implicit in the proofs of Kwok, Lau, and Tung [KLT22, KLT25],
although alternative algorithms are present in many other works for several steps.

Theorem 7.1 (Algorithmic form of Theorem 1.3). Let G be a graph with n vertices, m edges, and
maximum degree ∆. There is a randomized algorithm (succeeding w.h.p.) that runs in O((m2 + n3 ) ·
polylog(n)) time and outputs a set S with |S| ≤ n/2 and
                                        Æ
                                ψ(S) ≲ γ(n) (G) · min{log ∆, α(G)2 }.

There is also a randomized algorithm, succeeding with high probability, that runs in O((nm + n3 ) ·
polylog(n)) time and outputs a set T with |T | ≤ n/2 and
                                  Æ
                         ψ(T ) ≲ (γ(n) (G) + O(1/n)) · min{log ∆, α(G)2 }.

    As in previous work, there are two sub-steps in the algorithm: A dimension-reduction step and a
threshold rounding step.

Initial SDP solving. The input to the rounding step is a feasible and approximately optimal solution to
γ(n) (G), consisting of an embedding f : V → Rn and weights y : V → R≥0 satisfying

                                      y(u) + y(v) ≥ || f (u) − f (v)||22 ∀uv ∈ E.

Theorem 7.2 (Solving the vertex-expansion SDP). Let G be an n-vertex, m-edge graph. For every
ϵ > 0, one can compute a feasible solution for γ(n) (G) with objective value at most γ(n) (G) + ϵ in
O(m · polylog(n)/ϵ) time. One can also compute a feasible solution with objective value O(γ(n) (G)) in
O(m2 · polylog(n)) time.

     The additive error bound above follows by applying the Matrix Multiplicative Weights Update
method [AK16] to Roch’s formulation (see also [LTW24, Section 5.1]) and using approximate frac-
tional matchings, which can be obtained using a positive LP solver (e.g. Young’s [You14]). The mul-
tiplicative guarantee follows from the positive SDP solver of Jambulapati, Lee, Li, Padmanabhan, and
Tian [JLL+ 21].8 Our implementation uses the latter approach.
     To round, we perform two dimension-reduction steps, and take the best result of the two. One of
these dimension-reduction methods is quite slow in practice, so our implementation only uses the faster
one.

A    Graph Class Definitions
There are a number of graph classes that are relevant to our results. The most notable is the well-known
class of planar graphs, which are graphs that can be drawn on the plane without crossing edges. Most
of the other classes we will consider generalize planar graphs in some manner.

Definition A.1. A graph G is said to have genus g if it can be embedded into an orientable surface of
genus g, and it cannot be embedded into an orientable surface of smaller genus. Planar graphs are
exactly those that have genus 0.

    Up to homeomorphism, there is only one orientable surface of genus g, for any particular g. For
g = 0, it is the sphere. The orientable surface of genus g > 0 can be obtained from the orientable surface
of genus g − 1 by attaching a “handle”. Alternatively, it can be expressed as g “doughnuts” merged
together. See Figure 16 for examples of these surfaces, and see Figure 17 for an example of a graph
drawn on a genus-3 surface.




 Figure 16. Examples of (left to right) a genus-1 surface, a genus-2 surface, a genus-3 surface, and a genus 10 surface.




            Figure 17. An example of a graph (left) and a drawing of the graph on a genus-3 surface (right).


Definition A.2. For a graph G and a graph H, we say that H is a minor of G if it can be obtained from G
by a sequence of edge deletions, vertex deletions, and edge contractions (that is, merging the two vertices
incident to an edge into one). Equivalently, H is a minor of G if there are pairwise disjoint, nonempty
vertex sets X v ⊆ V (G) for v ∈ V (H) such that every G[X v ] is connected and, for each uv ∈ E(H), some
edge of G has one endpoint in X u and the other in X v .
    We say a graph G is H-minor-free if it does not contain H as a minor.

    We will also consider two much more fundamentally geometric graph classes:

Definition A.3. A graph G is said to be the intersection graph of d-dimensional balls with ply k
(sometimes called a d-dimensional k-ply neighbourhood system) if there is a collection of d-dimensional
balls B1 , . . . , Bn with ply at most k for all points almost surely, so that each ball corresponds to a vertex,
and each edge corresponds to a pair of vertices whose balls intersect.

Definition A.4. A d-dimensional k-nearest neighbour graph is a graph G with n vertices v1 , . . . , vn
corresponding to points p1 , . . . , pn ∈ Rd so that an edge vi v j exists if and only if pi is among the k-nearest
neighbours of p j , or p j is among the k-nearest neighbours of pi .
