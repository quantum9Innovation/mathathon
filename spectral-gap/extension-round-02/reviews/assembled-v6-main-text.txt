                   Centered permutation products:
               sharp envelopes and uniform asymptotics


                                     November 14, 2026


                                            Abstract
        We prove an attained envelope for mixed centered permutation products, allowing
    arbitrary multiplicities and independent colors. When no conflicting centered singleton
    occurs, compatible cells in one permutation attain the exact maximum for fixed support
    and singleton counts. Scalar consequences sharpen the probabilistic estimate used in
    random-lift and biregular spectral-gap arguments. For distinct diagonal cells, the leading
    terms of Collins and Nagatsu are relatively accurate exactly when k 3 /N → 0, answering
    their published optimal-scale question. The critical limits are parity-dependent hyperbolic
    functions. A positive partition expansion and an elementary saddle estimate give a
    uniform approximation throughout k = o(N ), a sharp hierarchy of correction thresholds,
    and an exact-denominator formula for every 2 ≤ k ≤ N . On bounded square-root ranges
    we determine the signed first error and the sharp constant in its uniform N −1/4 rate. We
    explain the spectral application and isolate two failures in an intermediate conditioning
    formula without asserting a new graph eigenvalue theorem.

1     The question and the proof mechanism
A permutation matrix has one nonzero entry in every row and column. Its entries are therefore
strongly dependent, even though each entry has mean 1/N . This dependence has a useful
consequence. Products of suitably separated centered entries are far smaller in expectation
than one would predict by taking absolute values first.

Two cells in the same row instead have centered product expectation −N −2 . The pattern of
requested cells matters. The smaller scale in (1) is cancellation, not independence.
    The mixed-product estimate in Bordenave’s Proposition 28 [1] exploits this distinction. It
also appears in Bordenave and Collins [5, Proposition 22]. Brito, Dumitriu and Harris import

proof built from three operations:
    1. Keep the isolated centered singletons together.
    2. Condition only on a compatible set of prescribed matches. The remaining matrix is
       exactly a smaller uniform permutation matrix.
    3. Use its row sum to obtain a positive recurrence, and only then expand the remaining
       factors.

8.1                              Random lifts: one permutation per base edge
In Bordenave’s model, choose one orientation of each undirected edge of a finite base graph.

8.3   Why isolated centered factors help a trace expansion
The non-backtracking matrix is indexed by oriented graph edges. Its entry from an oriented
edge e to an oriented edge f is one exactly when the head of e is the tail of f and f is not
the reverse of e. This definition permits a return along a different parallel edge. In a simple
graph, it reduces to v = x and y ≠ u for consecutive edges u → v and x → y. Thus powers of
the matrix count walks that never immediately reverse the same edge.
    The matrix is generally not normal. A safe norm estimate uses

                                     ∥C∥2s ≤ tr (CC ∗ )s .
                                                           


Expanding the right side for a centered path matrix produces products of matching entries. A
product that uses a distinct matches pstarts at scale L−a . Each consistent centered singleton
gives an additional factor of order a/L in our bound. Repeated cells and conflicting
singletons have different costs and must be counted separately.
    Choosing locations for a circuit contributes positive powers of the graph size. Matching
probabilities cancel most of them. Excess cycles and isolated centered factors supply the
savings that remain. The combinatorial argument must sum these estimates over all allowed
circuit shapes. The mixed-product theorem handles each fixed pattern, but does not perform
this sum.
    Short neighborhoods are typically tree-like, with a controlled allowance for a single cycle.
The papers restrict to such paths and estimate the remainder from more complicated local
tangles. Non-backtracking walks make this separation useful because immediate reversals
would create many large, uninformative contributions even on a tree.

10     Contribution, limitations and further questions
The mixed-product theorem identifies an attained maximum when there is no inconsistent
centered singleton. Its proof keeps the exact depleted moment, merges colors through positive
partition weights, and handles arbitrary multiplicities through affine indicator identities. The
scalar consequences recover the spectral estimate with a smaller singleton factor and an
optimal universal prefactor. The diagonal results determine the original cubic threshold, the
parity transition, a hierarchy of further corrections and the sharp first uniform error.
     The base recurrence, its normalized parity form and the leading coefficients belong to
Collins and Nagatsu [3]. The mixed estimate itself is already part of Bordenave’s spectral-gap
method [1]. The special-function identities and the saddle method are classical. The precise
refinements proved here are the attained envelope at fixed support and singleton counts,
the necessity and bounded-comparability characterization of the cubic scale, the uniform
sublinear correction hierarchy, and the sharp parity-dependent error on bounded square-root
ranges. A targeted literature check found no exact duplicate of these statements, but cannot
certify the absence of every earlier equivalent formula.                               √
     Bordenave and Zhu [9, Lemma 2.1] retain the original singleton factor 3k/ N and
                                                 √
explicitly bound the raw factorial ratio by e in their equation (2.3). That elementary
bound is a known ingredient here. Our exact refinement retains µh (n, a − h) in addition to
Fn (a − h). The factor Γn,a,h is a scalar upper bound on that moment and is smaller than β
when h < a. The attained-maximum assertion concerns b = 0. We do not claim that 2b is
sharp for prescribed b > 0.
     The Laguerre representation makes the parameter regime precise: the degree is k, the
parameter is α = −N − 1, and the argument is −N . Thus −α/k → ∞ throughout the
sublinear range. Kuijlaars and McLaughlin [10], and the regime described by Dai and Wong
[7], treat a finite limiting ratio greater than one. Those results overlap the macroscopic part
of our full-range formula, so that part is not presented as a new special-function asymptotic
regime. Theorem 1 of Dai, Ismail and Wang [8] assumes α > 0 when α/k → ∞. In the
inspected preprint of Dunster, Gil and Segura [11, Section 1, equation (1.6) and Cases 1a, 1b
and 2], α = (k + 1/2)(a2 − 1) with a ≥ 0, excluding α = −N − 1 for k ≤ N . These parameter
comparisons do not turn analytic continuation into a uniform error estimate.
     At k = N , the identity with a truncated exponential is classical, as recorded in [12,
Section 1]. The contribution of Theorem 6.4 is a self-contained formula that patches fixed
orders, the parity transition and the endpoint while keeping (N )k exact. Its stated O(N −1/4 )
global bound is not asserted to be optimal. The sharp rate in Section 7 concerns the different,
quartically corrected hyperbolic approximation.
     The inequalities are proved symbolically for their stated parameter ranges. Exact finite
computations check normalization, the depleted recurrence and the conditioning examples.
Numerical plots illustrate the asymptotic regimes. Neither kind of computation is used as a
substitute for a general proof.
     The remaining graph-level task is to combine the sharper cell-pattern estimate with circuit
enumeration and tangle remainders while retaining the degree dependence. A better estimate
for one fixed product does not automatically improve a spectral theorem: the patterns that
dominate the full sum must be identified first. The present paper does not assert a new
second-eigenvalue bound or an end-to-end audit of either random-graph proof.
